Andrew set these principles on 2026-09-26, after playing mobility kit v2. They are quoted verbatim in `docs/playtests/PLAYTEST-LOG.md` entry 6. They govern every Scrapkeep design call until a heavy-lane Bible amendment carries them.

1. **Momentum is sacred (Katamari Damacy).** The game is about rolling, building momentum and growing. Only the player's own performance costs momentum: a mistake, a collision, a hit taken. Nothing may inhibit the player in unforeseen ways. Collisions have weight. Loot, rewards and moving between areas happen on the move (drive-through, roll-through, launch).
2. **Quick to start, quick to restart.** A failed run returns to play in one keypress: from a checkpoint, the stage start, or a new run.
3. **Layers you opt into.** Maps have events the player can join for key milestone rewards. They can overlap with bosses.
4. **Growth that scales insanely,** like Binding of Isaac and Gunfire Reborn. Items transform weapons (cross shot, spiral waves). The balance rule is visual clarity, so the screen never overwhelms the player. **Synergies (added 2026-09-26):** he loves both accidental ones and explicit ones. They should be shown to exist, so they work as goals and create excitement, and they should be discoverable and make coherent sense, so the player wants to experiment. In the demo this became tag-built named synergies shown as hint cards in a codex, a discovery moment saved across runs, and unnamed system interactions logged as "Found".
5. **Class identities.** Examples: a pylon engineer who acts as a placeable turret and uses the terrain, and a runner-gunner with rapid, tight laser fire or AOE.
6. **Music.** **The ACE-Step hybrid wins (2026-09-28):** in the blind A/B he picked the ACE-Step drum-and-keys bed under the live engine layers ("I really like the ace step version ... I love it"), then asked to tighten the production, which sounded "a bit disjointed and choppy ... towards the middle". The likely cause is the per-bar retime, which left bars 7 to 9 and 14 off the grid. What he wants from the music: "layered melodies ... that kind of pop in and out with different sets of like percussion or ... instrumental ... harmony ... and flow with ... understandable switch-ups based on the flow of the game or the area that you're in." So each area or state gets its own percussion set and harmony layer, with melodies entering and leaving on game events. **Restraint (2026-09-28):** the tightened hybrid failed: "a freaking mess ... zero restraint and just like ten layers of tracks layered on top of each other". It had doubled drum kits (a synth floor under the cover) and nearly every live layer on. The rule now: one drum kit at a time, a small cap on simultaneous layers, and bringing a layer in means taking one out. Density must be earned by game state, never the default. **Groove lock (2026-09-28):** the two-layer restrained render also failed ("the bassline and the percussion ... sound off ... doesn't mesh well at all"). The ACE-Step cover's drums played a different groove from the engine's bass, and B's density had only masked it. Lesson: three rounds of post-processing one cover can't fix a pattern mismatch. Fix it at generation (a drums-only guide, higher cover strength, a kick and bass-groove screen before retiming), and let his blind listen pick, never the automatic score. **Full ACE-Step wins (2026-09-28):** blind pack 2 held three full ACE-Step performances (its own bass and drums, screened for grid, downbeats, key, density and bass-kick lock) against B: "Much, much better ... it was just uh hybrid approach that just did not mix". He then clarified "not X4 ... one, two, and three are great"; X4 was B, the hybrid, so blind, all three full-ACE takes (a-080-s1001, c-085-s7, c-090-s7) beat the hybrid. The direction: the engine composes guides, ACE-Step performs whole tracks, and experimentation is welcome ("shoot for the stars ... having fun with it"). **Pack 3 and judging in play (2026-09-28):** he liked all three 3-minute play-flow tracks and slightly preferred Y3, which was the planted "weak" take: raw ACE dynamics, the one the screens rated worst. His insight: "if you're going to be playing, and like fighting something, then those nuances will probably get lost ... in the moment to moment experiences", so judge music from the player's perspective. The rules that follow:
- Screens only reject broken takes; they never rank.
- Subtle arrangement serves the overall vibe, and moments that carry game information (the Warden opening, the drop) must be unmistakable. They double as audio cues.
- Final music calls are made in the game with the fight on, not in passive listening packs.
- **The exception is directed set pieces:** "something that is more cinematic ... directed ... from point A to B ... so you're forced to go at the tempo of the direction". Composed, nuanced passages belong in game-paced moments (the Warden break into the kill cadence, the return lift, cannon and trebuchet flights, stage intros, the bay entrance).
- **Parked idea:** in those set pieces, the game follows the music. For example, contraption flight times land you on the downbeat.

High BPM, energizing EDM drive with jazzy, inventive melodies. On 2026-09-26 he called the first 172 BPM D Dorian band preview "amazing". He then asked for level themes (instrument palette, rhythm and melody per level). He also asked for boss music in three tiers: miniboss, level boss and final boss. Boss music should borrow from its level's theme, and the final boss gets his own full set. The style is epic, funky, jazzy, thumping, dance-floor Euro club and electro. It is recorded in DEMO-SPEC's Music section. Brindle's phases quote the stage motifs in reverse order. After the second round he called the music "fantastic" and asked for a bass line that runs through everything, is rhythmic, and drops at the perfect moment, with a 2011–12 electro and dubstep feel. The answer ties each drop to game physics: the launch rail is the build and touchdown is the drop; breaking the Warden and each Brindle phase are drops; a slam sounds an instant sub-bass womp. He loves the Warden Spillway transition (the triplet pivot into 129), and it is the benchmark. He also loves tempo and key shifts tied to boss phases, moving through sinister and intense ("great layering… gets me so immersed"). As built, the Warden's music escalates as it breaks: a key lift at 50%, then a sinister break at 75%. Brindle's arc goes Phrygian, then accelerates, and his chase returns to the exploration tempo of 172. After round 3 (the bass pass) he said to rein back the wubs: no talking growls, and little wobble. He loves the underlying bass lines, layered and complementary. His reference is the first half of Frank Ocean's "Pyramids": melodies layered one at a time, re-textured, thrown back in and remixed as the song progresses. Distil that style; never copy the song. After round 4 (the gems pass) he said "warden spill B is fire" and asked to apply its approach to every OST track and game sound. B's grammar is the standing rule: keep each track's loved core intact; escalate on real game-state thresholds (the Warden goes key lift at 50% broken, sinister break at 75% tightening per hit, drop home on the unseal); add layers, never swap them in; land the payoff on the gameplay moment. SFX follow the same grammar in miniature and always stay in the live key, including lifted keys. The gem ledger is `sketches/core-loop/src/presentation/MUSIC-GEMS.md`. Check it before any music change, so nothing he loved is lost. **v2 audio identity (2026-09-27):** `sketches/core-loop/SOUND-STORY.md` holds the leitmotifs, all in D:
   - Sable: a rising Dm9 on mallets.
   - Rivet: a hammered note then a fourth, on hat and clav.
   - Kest: a falling triad on tom and sub.
   - The Fleet: an anvil plus a brass D–E♭–D.
   - The ancients: a reactor tritone pedal, a swarm buzz that locks into a quartal chord, and the Mind quoting the class motif inverted and wrong.

   **2026-10-19:** songs must develop: the motif returns changed, the song arcs over 2 to 3 minutes, and nothing sounds stitched or overlaid (DECISIONS.md, PR #201). Every mini-boss, boss and intense event gets a bass-heavy EDM anthem in the style of Swedish House Mafia, Avicii and Basshunter: a singable hook, a build, a drop and a bigger second drop, with jazz or Latin twists welcome. Roaming music stays jazzy (PR #202). The older wub limits and Warden Spill B grammar still apply.

   His rulings: only the reactor colours the band, and the Fleet borrows Brindle's anvil and brass one layer per difficulty tick. The Mind's inverted quote is the one sanctioned hostile use of a class motif. The ancients' light is Cherenkov blue.
7. **Harmonize apparent contradictions.** Break a tension down until one design serves both sides. Example: the pylon banks momentum and then slingshots the player back out. Actions should be snappy or fluid, so the player feels like a badass.
9. **Focused, coherent mechanics (added later on 2026-09-26).** The roguelite must gate and channel the player through its stages, whether that is vertical, open or something else. The player must engage with some things and should engage with others. Some adversaries are too strong until the player has powered up. Mechanics must reinforce one another rather than sprawl. Andrew asked for agents to consolidate ideas, reject biases and reconcile decisions against anti-patterns as a standing practice over a long-horizon session.
8. **Rigid physics.** Andrew picked it in every play. It reads as weighty and arcadey; kinematic felt squishy. Not realistic, fun.
10. **Gating spine (Andrew decided 2026-09-26, `sketches/core-loop/DEMO-SPEC.md`).**
   - Growth is heft: mass from scrap plus parts. Spending never shrinks it, only a telegraphed theft does, and it never changes handling.
   - The demand is punch against plating. A hit below plating chips for 20%, so there is no hard wall.
   - One Warden clamps each beacon and holds the stage's core. Breaking it is the single MUST.
   - Events and vaults drop bonus cores, which overcharge the reward gates.
   - Growth shows in the shell; the collider stays the same.
   - Brindle has fixed HP and is fought with the same grammar.
   - Rejected: key or fuse checklists, kill-all gates, and boss HP scaling.
   - He took the recommended option every time. Ask the rival question at the next playtest.

11. **Readability and v2 direction (entry 7, 2026-09-27, after playing the core-loop demo).**
   - The UI must say at a glance what to press, what hurts and how to grow. Use icons and symbols over words, and time information to the moment it's needed. Stopping to read defeats the game.
   - Telegraphs must tell instant AOE, travelling waves and projectiles apart, and trace where things will hit.
   - Menus take the mouse and have transitions.
   - Keep: the gunning ("feels great"), the difficulty tuning, and the Risk of Rain stay-longer-gets-harder pressure. He likes to self-challenge with little gear or against a timer.
   - **Three characters, each with its own movement.**
     - Engineer: booms, bounces and turrets; grapple and vault.
     - Runner-gunner: unchanged; an unstoppable sprint.
     - Explosive demolitions caster: skill shots, hides, bursts; glide.
     - Everyone gets a ground smash with a per-character flavour.
   - **Combat feel.** A bowling-ball smash-and-grab, rebounding off enemies. A wall vault roughly doubles momentum. League-style hold-gauge-release skill shots give forgiveness.
   - **Cooldowns and loot.** Fast cooldowns that still need timing, with items that stockpile charges. Procedural loot in the Isaac, Risk of Rain and Gunfire Reborn style.
   - **Standardized systems, so there are no hidden bugs.** Accidental exploits that are fun are welcome.
   - **Maps.** Exploratory, vertical 3D maps: underground, aerial, floating hovercraft.
   - **Enemies: creativity, never reskins.** A pet peeve: "skins of the same enemy that uh, does the same mechanic." Every enemy type owns a mechanic that changes what the player must do. Staying longer brings reinforcements with anti-ball counters: mag pits, tethers, air nets, bolas, plated brutes, deflectors and pullers. Their crowd control can chain across a bad combination of enemies. Those chains are telegraphed, breakable and capped by tenacity. The enemy origin, chosen 2026-09-27: the Furnace Fleet's excavations woke pre-apocalypse ancient war machines, which caused the collapse. Fleet crews are the reinforcements with anti-ball gear. The ancients are the wild threats and the Wardens. **The ancients are us:** humans built those machines, and the collapse came from nuclear war, drone technology or superintelligence, or a mix. That gives three ancient families (reactor relics, swarm remnants, the Mind's hands), each with a unique mechanic. The true cause stays ambiguous and is pieced together through codex fragments. **The characters:** "life finds a way". After humans were lost, animals became the reigning intelligent species. That matches the Bible's candidate pilots: Sable the fox, Kest the bird, Rivet the mole, and Brindle the badger. Whether they evolved or something humans left behind uplifted them stays ambiguous. The theme is salvagers repurposing the tech that ended us, while the Fleet digs up what should have stayed buried.
   - **Contraptions and a changing world.** Steam lifts and launchers, maps built around a focal landmark, and events that change the terrain: cave-ins, craters, collapses. The look is post-apocalyptic futurism.
   - **Calm before the storm (2026-09-27).** He likes a lull between phases that he chooses, with real shop decisions. Moss's workshop is an opt-in side bay after each Warden break. It banks speed, and its music is a sax-led lounge version of the stage theme. It offers a shelf of 3 parts, a reroll, charge upgrades and sell-back. The difficulty clock pauses inside. It carries his "Potion Seller" meme homage: Moss withholds the top-shelf part until the ball's heft class is high enough. His lines are original, never the sketch's script.
   - **The ball is the culture (2026-09-27).** Maps are built for the ball first: bowls, banks, marble-maze runs, racetracks, cannons and trebuchets. The pilots' societies are nomadic momentum tribes who build camps up and tear them down. The gyroscopic balls are the survival form, built on gyros salvaged from ancient machines. Enemies evolved counters to balls: gyro jammers that disorient, swatters, ramp-flippers and catch-traps. They develop new counters as the difficulty clock climbs, and they test the build the player chose, never hard-counter it.
   - **The kit is a binary upgrade tree** chosen at checkpoints between stages (Moss's workshop visits): 2, then 4, then 8, then 16 end variations, covering mobility and everything else. Its exact shape is still to confirm.
   - **Moss's bay is a hangout.** He loves the babble and the club vibe. The bay gets a practice yard, fidget toys and a small mini-game.
   - **Bosses are epic by design, and the run is a hero's journey (2026-09-27, after the first Warden model).** He rejected a Warden that "doesn't really look like a boss". Wardens must be menacing, intimidating and "really cool", epic in design rather than scale, and their form must show their main mechanics. He wants real effort on enemy design. The run is a hero's journey, "being versus God": the odds look insurmountable, never actually unfair, and dare the player to take on the challenge. Each Warden is a rung up toward the godlike. The design lives in `design-v2/WARDEN-DESIGN.md`. He liked the V3 shells and wants idle, pulse and active animation on them. He is considering a true sphere mech with a visible pilot (the "Super Monkey Ball" read, more epic) and gyroscopic rings, where each pilot's race shapes its machinery. He wants an **unlockable fourth pilot**: a quirky, glitchy, friendly ancient robot as comic relief, who transmogs to match the abilities and tech it holds. The design lives in `design-v2/HERO-SPHERE.md`.
   - **A parallel look track.** A visual language, then icons, then Blender hero assets, all in keeping with the theme, with "more soul and character".

12. **Engagement over polish (entry 8, 2026-09-28, after the v2 alpha candidate).** He won the demo by beelining and called it "a half-baked attempt": the map must matter, and the Warden must say when and where to hit, whether you dodged, and how close it is to breaking. Priority is map design and gameplay first, visuals later. Iterate the loop fast in greybox. Keep: movement (rolling, flying, jumping, glide, Rivet's sprint) and the enemy attacks, which were "pretty cool and pretty fun". The fantasy is a "love project" in the family of Isaac, Gunfire Reborn, Risk of Rain, Hades and Vampire Survivors: "crazy chaotic mess" and "unique run capability" for one more run. He wants a universal interact key for consenting to travel devices, opting into fights, secrets and treasure, switching on temporary allies, and risk bets that hit or miss on luck or skill. **His feel notes are direction, not specs:** "juicy feedback" and "eye candy" that make him want "to know what's gonna happen next with like these upgrades", with "more creativity and discovery". Build anticipation, such as teases of what a part becomes, and let players learn by experimenting rather than reading. Also wanted: a class card that sells the pilot, and a Tab page that is a picture, not an essay. See [[fun-gate-before-alpha]].

13. **Subtract to add (2026-09-28).** When design sprawls, trim: one canonical design doc instead of a pile, delete losing variants in code rather than keeping them switchable, build the minimum high-impact set first and defer the rest, fewer parallel threads, and archive-then-remove finished worktrees. Codex (Astra) takes design and review load so the Claude session limit goes to building; he authorized heavy Codex use and Astra at high effort.

**Why:** He described these as his biggest pet peeves and his core fantasy as a player. The whole point of the game is building momentum.

**How to apply:** Check every mechanic, transition and UI flow against principle 1 first. Flag any design where the game, rather than the player, stops the ball. Ask Andrew only when a call would change the direction of the deliverable. Otherwise decide within these principles and log the call in the sketch's `DESIGN-NOTES.md`. Related: [[jev-lab-close-and-game-dev-start]], [[flag-project-model-pins-before-spending]].
14. **Propagate a new mechanic through every system it touches (confirmed 2026-09-28).** When the pin polymorph replaced the dancing Jig, he praised three things: bosses resist it with a visible "nope" cue that leaves their windows untouched; it plugs into existing systems (the pin rule, the lanes' Strike, Spare and Turkey, and Pinball Hull); and the reaction was renamed Bowl Over to avoid clashing with the Strike callback. For every new mechanic, check its boss resistance, the callbacks and systems it could feed, name collisions, the control caps, and how it reads on screen.
15. **Animation pipeline (2026-09-28).** He loves the mesh → Astra rig → ImageGen keyframes (16 keys, each with front, back and side views) → Astra animation pipeline. Rivet's drop-landing reaction was the first run. His bar: "it doesn't have to be overly complex ... it just has to be yeah, good enough". Favour big body shapes that read at chase distance over facial detail. Use one shared rig bone schema across pilots. Enemy wind-ups map the keys onto the telegraph, strike and recovery phases. **Pilots react to play (2026-09-28):** "the pilot should react to the player's uh, actions, achievements, or failures". Build it as we go: one reaction table (event, clip, priority, cooldown) read from one source, clips made through the keyframe pipeline, and never spammy. **Timing fits the action (2026-09-28):** "you don't want to ... rush things and have it be a movement that's ... not really perceivable". Length follows what the action has to communicate. Every beat needs enough screen time to read at chase distance, key poses get short holds, and only the impact itself is fast.
16. **Look and performance target (2026-09-28).** Iterate the look to 80 to 90 percent of the Style A concept art, meaning look and feel only (lighting, materials, palette, mood, silhouettes); UI or layouts drawn in the art are not specs. It must hold 60 FPS at 1080p on his Ryzen 9 5950X with an RTX 4080 and on mid-tier PCs ("I wouldn't want this game to be something that a mid-tier card or PC would be having a hard time to play"). Proxy budgets on the dev box for the densest fight: CPU main thread p99 ≤ 10 ms, GPU p99 ≤ 7 ms. Performance matters to him; treat it as a gate on every visual change, not a last wave.
17. **Feedback and discovery (2026-09-28).** For a game about timing, dodging and reading 3D space, attacks read through the attacker first. Every attacker (enemies, Wardens, the player) announces a hit with body and sound: wind-up, charge, strike, recovery. The floor marking becomes support, a thin translucent red haze or projection line drawn from the committed damage geometry. First discoveries of a synergy, item, interactable or reaction get a picture card: a looping animation of what it does, a name, a few words, and the ceremony's calm gate (pause only when calm, otherwise in place, and any input skips). Repeats get a quick glyph-plus-name pop. Unlocks and completed tree branches get a celebration tier. The codex is browsable from the home menu, with descriptions, synergies and interactions. He loves double-edged items with tremendous upside and a real downside that can make or ruin a run, and mutated starting seeds. The working name is "hot-wired parts", since he finds "cursed" cliché. Canonical text: v3 ROADMAP, "Feedback and discovery".
    **Superseded 2026-10-16/17:** the alpha is now the full game (worlds 1 to 3) as a finished-feeling Linux alpha by wave 21, judged only by Andrew's 11-area sitting ([[play-verdict-batched]]). The 28 Sep scope below is history.
    **Alpha scope (2026-09-28): introductory level.** The alpha is the start of the game, world one up to its boss, so all content is pitched at what a first-time player meets there. In the alpha: attack reads for world one's enemies and Warden, first-discovery cards for world one's content, and 2 or 3 gentle hot-wired parts in wave D. After the alpha: progression ceremonies, the home-menu codex, more hot-wired parts and mutated seeds (v3 7c9fa68).
18. **Scope lock: the end of world one is the definition of done (2026-09-28).** World one is stages 1 to 3, their Wardens, and Brindle, the world boss. Flesh out every system in full detail to the depth a player reaches by Brindle and no deeper; for example, only the kit-tree tiers reachable in world one, fully detailed per pilot, not the 16 end paths. Assets and animation can be tweaked in place. At the milestone: reassess, re-baseline, then set direction (v3 ROADMAP 060b917).
19. **Scrap is size; every system earns its place (2026-09-28).** He didn't see the point of the scrap wallet (it gave no decisions). Ruling: scrap is your size, the wallet goes, and scrap gets a thematic, understandable benefit. The lead candidate is durability: scrap is armour, and hits knock it loose to roll back up. Heft-class thresholds transform the ball: it grows and details layer on, with a ceremony beat, while the collider and handling stay the same, and losing a class sheds a layer. Judge every system on the dimensions it serves: strategy, risk, gambling, performance and skill, personal-best mastery, expression, discovery, feel, pacing, momentum cost and readability cost. Name the decision it gives the player; merge or cut systems that serve none. The Codex purpose audit (task-mulp911v-yt4lih) feeds DESIGN-V3 in wave B (v3 aa1b0e2, 14336ab).
    **Durability settled (2026-09-28, v3 103d206):** scrap is temporary health over the hull. Hits knock it loose to roll back up, and rolling friction slowly wears it, which pulls toward aggressive collecting. Wear never touches the hull. Class-down has hysteresis so the transformation can't flicker. **One pilot, several ways to play:** Sable can be a scrap-hungry juggernaut, a ninja platformer, or a wall-launched cannonball (the Indiana Jones boulder). World one's kit tiers give each pilot at least two distinct play identities.
20. **Mass is stored momentum (2026-09-28, v3 ROADMAP).** Handling stays size-neutral (he kept the spine rule). Each pilot's kit tree cashes mass into their own verb: Sable's rebounds and slingshot gain speed with size, "a beam of rolling big boulder rock"; Rivet's smash and Kest's payload are extensions to test. Bulk penalties exist only on opt-in hot-wired parts, and pilot lines can cancel or flip them. The general pattern is inversion, a skill that turns a state's cost into its upside. **He wants every idea he gives extended one abstraction up into its general pattern, not just recorded.**
21. **Cogs: currency as agency against RNG (2026-09-28, v3 f436f3f; Andrew delegated the design).** Scrap is what you wear; cogs are what you spend. Cogs burst in volume and are vacuumed by a magnet that grows with heft. Loot is common, and any unwanted part or skipped pick recycles into cogs; hot-wired parts recycle rich, which makes "take the money" a gamble. Cogs buy staples on Moss's shelf, fabricator hoops, rerolls, holds and tag steering. Pools are seeded per world with bad-luck protection. It builds in wave D.
22. **State cues live on the part that carries the state (2026-09-29).** The plated enemies' "your punch beats this plate now" cue flooded the whole body with class-colour glow, which Codex failed as a pale wash. Andrew chose to move the cue to the plate's rim, bolts and crack glyph, in the shared enemy path, so the finish stays oxide and the cue stays bold. General pattern: a state read goes on the feature it describes, never a full-body tint. A finish fix must never delete a gameplay read; when they conflict, move the read.
    **ACE in-game rulings (2026-09-28):** the held-breath default is **hard** (drums and bass out, -32 dB), because the Warden opening must be unmistakable. Raw, his pack-3 favourite, measured -0.1 dB and barely read as a cue; it stays one key away (B, or the pause menu's Breath row). The Warden-break set piece **plays through the launch flight and hands over at the landing** ("directed A to B"), rather than cutting to the synth at launch.
23. **The harpoon and winch is the shared verb (2026-09-30, v3 ROADMAP 4a26e58).** Every pilot gets a right-click harpoon on a winch as the game's identity. From the first run you harpoon an enemy and winch yourself in for a directed smash, with the risk of landing in the crowd. Pulling, dragging, whipping and throwing enemies are earned through upgrades, items and cross-run unlocks. The pattern: a line joins two bodies, the winch shortens it, and the looser end travels. Each pilot sends their own thing along the line: Sable motion, Rivet force, Kest payloads. At the Warden it is a drop finisher, never a bowl hammer. Co-op griefing is parked. The prototype is flag-gated and played after the lab sitting.
24. **The world is a natural, uncanny jungle gym, and enemies have home turf (2026-10-02).** His standing complaint: the world feels hollow, "alien objects and scaffolding ... geometric shapes", like Play-Doh sculpted, not natural. He wants landforms that feel natural yet are fun to traverse many ways, "an uncanny jungle gym", with enemies throughout that spawn from places integrated with the environment so they have a home advantage. His muse is Arizona's geography: national-park trails, natural landmarks and iconography. Working mapping (mine, for his veto): slot canyons are flow tubes, slickrock is a skatepark, washes are halfpipes, hoodoo and spire forests and saguaros are harpoon anchors, Horseshoe Bend is a banked turn, travertine terraces are a drop line, Meteor Crater is a Warden bowl, cliff dwellings are enemy homes, Grand Canyon strata suit the Dig, Monument Valley buttes suit the Highdraft. **How to apply:** every landform carries a traversal verb and a home for an enemy kind; no bare primitives or scaffolding as terrain; enemy spawns come from homes whose shape gives that kind its advantage and the player a readable counter.
25. **Every hit has weight, and its sight and sound land together (2026-10-11, taste note, by phone mid-wave 13).** "Make sure abilities and effects have impact and weight and that pairs the visual with the audio as well," for the hero pilots and for the enemy and Warden classes alike. **How to apply:** an ability, attack, hit, stagger, break or death reads its weight from one shared record per event kind, and that record drives hit-stop, shake, VFX size and the sound layer on the same tick, so the layers cannot drift; heavier events get strictly more of each, and a heavy hit never sounds or looks like a light one. Impact never hides a telegraph (readability first). Builds on the "slam sounds an instant sub-bass womp" rule (item 6) and the Spill B grammar for SFX. First applied in the wave-13 impact audit (wf_fb3e9f36-a3c) and folded into every later pilot-kit, pilot-clip and Warden brief.

**Tune harder, never easier (Andrew, 2026-11-10, dictated mid-gate).** "This is your code base, so feel free to tune the difficulty. I think the harder the better": fine if "only the most capable players can one-shot or beat the level ... skillfully", assuming they are "very practiced"; it should be "tuned to be more difficult rather than easier". He likes Isaac's difficulty. The fun he names: "rolling around and flailing", "Tarzan or bowling ball steamroll my way through enemies or around them and kite them"; kiting as in Isaac and Risk of Rain 2. My reading (label: mine): the Novice win-rate ceiling may drop and is no longer a reason to park a change that makes play harder; a change that makes play easier needs a compensating pressure; Expert must still be able to win; difficulty comes from enemies and pressure that reward moving, kiting and ramming, never from stopping the ball ([[scrapkeep-design-principles]] item 1). The coordinator tunes without asking and logs the call. Same day he gave ten map design points and asked for a fast code or picture check for each; they are in DECISIONS (map standard ruling) and `~/scratch/world1/map-audit.md`.
