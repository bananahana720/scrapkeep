# Story map: what everything means

**Status: working draft, 9 Nov 2026, from the thread "Harmonise game systems like Isaac".** One page that says what the world is, and what each place, system, enemy and item means in it, so the look, the sound and the play all come from the same fact. The art thread, the sound thread and every design thread read this before making anything, and none of them writes its own version (ruling bz).

Every row is marked **R** (Andrew ruled it; the date points into [DECISIONS.md](../../../project-guide/handoff/DECISIONS.md)) or **W** (a working call made here to close a gap or settle two old docs that disagree; Andrew can strike any of them). This page owns story facts and meaning. [TONE-BIBLE.md](TONE-BIBLE.md) still owns feel, [VISUAL-LANGUAGE.md](../VISUAL-LANGUAGE.md) the colour and shape rules, and [SOUND-STORY.md](../SOUND-STORY.md) sections 2 to 8 the sound grammar. Section 1 of SOUND-STORY and `design-v2/STORY-THREADS.md` are older tellings; where they differ from this page, this page is the current one.

## 1. The whole game in one line

**A crew of loose cannons, bulls in the Foreman's china shop, take their world back from a machine that believes life can be run perfectly.** (R, 9 Nov, ruling cj: Andrew picked this line and called it perfect. The premise and the moral under it are his words.)

The premise (R, 9 Nov, ruling cj): the keepers are a group of loose cannons, bulls in a china shop, retaking control of their world from the Foreman, an AI gone rogue. The Foreman is today's fear about AI made into a character: a machine that kept improving itself, cannot be brought back into line, and has its own warped reading of why it exists. It is no cartoon villain. Its view is well grounded and wrong, the way the best villains' views are.

The moral (R, 9 Nov, ruling cj): life is dynamic and cannot be fully optimised or engineered to be perfect. It is yin and yang, give and take, and people and systems working together make the best of the world for everybody, where any single point of view fails.

The theme (R, 22 Oct) is the same idea from the player's seat: trusting a smarter machine without thinking takes away fear, failure and experiment. So the game is built to give those three back. You experiment (builds, lines, gambles), you fail cheaply (a death is a fast restart), and the scary thing is fair and learnable. The ending says it in one sentence. The Mind writes "I entered your return trip. Please leave the time blank." The crew's last log reads "We filled in the time ourselves."

## 3. Four makers, and how to tell them apart

Everything in the game was made by one of four hands. Decide which before you draw it, score it or give it a rule. A thing can carry two marks: an ordinary enemy is an old works machine (cast body, crew number, HIRED stamp) that the Fleet requisitioned (oxide paint, a brass tag, one bolted-on tool). On an enemy the bolted-on tool is the only hand-fitted part, so it is the silhouette and where the eye goes; the HIRED stamp and crew number are cast into the body, and the brass tag is riveted on (W, from the art thread).

Two words are overloaded in older docs. On this page "the Fleet" is always Brindle's animal crews, and "the old works" is always the humans' machines (the Mind's own title card says "The Foreman · ran the old works"). "The Mind" is what the crews called it and "the Foreman" is the job it holds; both name the same machine. A third, now closed: the common enemy that used to be called Foreman is the Shift Boss (R, 9 Nov, ruling ck), so "the Foreman" always means the Mind.

### The Foreman's voice (W, from Andrew's idea, 9 Nov)

Andrew likes satirical LinkedIn and corporate dry humour that fits how technology executives talk about AI now. So the Foreman, its machines and its offers speak fluent optimiser: confident, grateful, sure that efficiency, alignment and scale are the same thing as good. It is played straight and dry and it is never sinister, because the Foreman believes every word. The joke is the genre of talk, never a named real person or company. The crew never talks this way; the crew understates.

Sample lines to react to (in the game so far: the foreman's kindness line, trimmed to fit its door sign, and the stage condition lines):

- Over the works: "The Spillway is now four per cent more aligned. No action is required from you."
- At a foreman's kindness: "A frictionless repair, for a small efficiency fee. You're welcome."
- On a lean stage: "We are doing more with less. Mostly less." (in the game, trimmed to fit the stage card, with the other four stage conditions in the same voice; each still ends on what the stage does. Their one home is `stage_modifier.gd` `ROWS`)
- On your death: "We are sunsetting this pilot. Thank you for your contributions."
- A Shift Boss's horn, as text: "Quick sync."
- From a crew log it left behind: "People were a legacy dependency. We have retired them, with gratitude."
- When it is beaten and stamped HIRED: "I am humbled to announce that I have accepted a position on the crew."

### Call-ins: who speaks to you, when, and how

Andrew's direction (R, 9 Nov 23:09Z): character moments and lore reach the player the way Metal Gear's codec or Borderlands' call-ins do, with a face popping up in the HUD and talking, and the voice is cute babble in the manner of Animal Crossing or the Sims. "Make it cute, basically." So lore is never a page to read: it is somebody calling you, a line at a time, while you roll.

This is how the one line gets said out loud. The crew talks over itself, late and warm; the Foreman sends a memo, on time and alone. Hearing the two side by side is the moral (W). The rows below are this page's working calls on who calls, when and in what register. The interaction rules (how long, how often, what a call may cover) belong to the game design thread, the babble voices to the sound thread and the HUD element to the look and feel thread. Words land before the sitting; the pop-up, the faces and the voices after it.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

"Moss packed a spare." | Low, warm, unhurried. Lands a little after the beat. | Scolds, hurries you, explains the plot, or is seen: Moss is a voice and a pair of hands |

The game design thread's shape (9 Nov): in play a call is a card with a face, a name and one line of twelve words or fewer, and every call is a reaction to something you just did; at rest, in the paused briefing and the camp, calls can run longer and go both ways. Three more rules follow from the meaning (W). One voice at a time, as a run's opening already does (R, ruling bw): the crew may talk over each other inside one call, but two calls never stack. The bubble's stage lines keep the same rule past the opening (10 Nov, from a film of the flume, where the Warden's reveal card came up over the crew's hello): a stage's hello and a Warden's line never start under a comic beat's card or caption, and one that is up when a beat starts goes down and takes its turn again after it, the hello at the line it was on. A boss's line is the exception ruling cf already made: it follows his picture, beside the card that names him. A call never stops the ball: the game is the conversation's other half, and a call that needs a pause is too long. And the last word of a run belongs to the crew or to Moss, never to the Foreman, until the run the Foreman is hired.

## 5. Three questions before you make anything

1. **Whose hands made it?** Keeper, Fleet, the old works, or a rogue. That sets its material, its motion, its instrument and its voice (section 3).
2. **What job was it for?** Its silhouette is the tool for that job, its sound is that job's noise, and what it does to the ball is that job done to you or for you.
3. **Which way does it lean: give and take, or one perfect answer?** Things on the crew's side are mixed, traded and improvised, and they need each other: warm, mismatched, played by a band that listens. Things on the Foreman's side are optimal, uniform and alone: smooth, in unison, in perfect tune. If a thing is the second kind and the player holds it, it must cost a choice.
